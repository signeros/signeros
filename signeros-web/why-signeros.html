An honest comparison

Why.
SignerOS

There are six reasonable ways to hold your own bitcoin, and SignerOS is better than the others at three things and worse at four. This page says which, in a table you can argue with.

▲ WHO WROTE THIS

The author of one of the six. That is a real bias and no disclaimer removes it, so here is the practical version: every claim in the SignerOS column is checkable against the source code, and the rows where it comes last are marked in red rather than left out. If you find a row that flatters it unfairly, that is a bug worth reporting.

Nothing here is a ranking. A setup that is wrong for €200 is right for €200,000, and the reverse.

01The six

What each one actually is

All six are self-custody: you hold the key, nobody can freeze your coins, and nobody will help you if you lose your backup. They differ in what has to be trusted while a transaction is being signed.

Offline PC + Bitcoin Core

Air-gapped · full node workflow

A machine that never goes online runs Bitcoin Core and signs PSBT files; a second machine with your own node builds and broadcasts them.

Best at: trusting nobody. Your own node validates the rules, your own software builds the transaction, no third-party server sees your addresses.

The cost: two machines, a synced node, hundreds of gigabytes, and a general-purpose OS whose networking is disabled only by your discipline.

Tails + Electrum

Amnesic live OS · offline signing

Boot Tails from a stick, run Electrum in offline mode, sign a transaction exported from an online Electrum, and carry it back.

Best at: maturity per euro. Tails and Electrum are both years old, widely used and reviewed by people with no stake in them.

The cost: Tails is built to reach the internet through Tor. The network stack is there, so the air gap is a rule you keep, not a property of the system.

SignerOS

Air-gapped by construction · RAM only

A USB image that turns a computer you already own into a signer with one job. No network code is compiled into the kernel at all, and nothing survives power-off.

Best at: removing choices. There is nothing to configure, no browser, no drives visible, nothing to leave behind, and nothing to buy or ship.

The cost: new and unaudited, no secure element, no PIN, and you type your recovery words at the start of every session.

Hot wallet on a PC

Online · keys on the machine

Sparrow, Electrum or similar running on your everyday computer, with the key stored on its disk.

Best at: capability. The full desktop toolset — coin control, labelling, multisig coordination, your own node — with nothing to plug in.

The cost: the key lives on an online, general-purpose machine. Any malware that reaches your user account can wait for you to unlock the wallet.

Hot wallet on a phone

Online · hardware-backed storage

A mobile wallet holding its own key, protected by the phone's screen lock and its hardware keystore.

Best at: actually being used. Fast, in your pocket, hard to lose, and the platform is patched more reliably than most desktops.

The cost: always online, dozens of other apps, and a screen you cannot fully trust once the device is compromised.

Hardware wallet

Purpose-built · key never leaves

A small device that keeps the key inside it for life and returns signatures over USB, SD or QR.

Best at: physical security. A secure element on most models, a PIN, wipe-after-N-attempts, tamper-evident packaging and a display that does one thing.

The cost: you buy it, so somebody makes it and somebody ships it. Firmware openness varies, secure-element code is usually closed, and the vendor is a party you trust.

03Where it loses

Four places SignerOS is the weakest of the six

If you only read one section of this page, read this one. A comparison written by an author is only worth anything where it goes against the author.

✕ NOBODY HAS AUDITED IT

It is new, it was written by one person, and no external security review has been done. Bitcoin Core, Electrum and the established hardware vendors have years of adversarial attention that this project simply has not had. Reproducible builds are not a substitute for review — they prove the binary matches the source, not that the source is right.

✕ NO SECURE ELEMENT, NO PIN

A hardware wallet is built to be captured: PIN, wipe-after-N-attempts, a chip designed to resist someone with the device on a bench. SignerOS has none of that, because it is software on a computer. Its answer is that the stick holds no key at all — which is a different design, not a stronger one. Everything rests on your paper backup and, if you use one, your passphrase.

✕ THE HARDWARE IS NOT UNDER ITS CONTROL

It boots on an ordinary PC, and the UEFI firmware, the management engine and anything implanted in the machine all sit below the operating system. Secure Boot support is in the build but has never been tested on real firmware, and the released images are unsigned on purpose. An old laptop with no valuables in it is the sensible way to run this.

✕ YOU TYPE YOUR WORDS EVERY TIME

Nothing persists, which is the feature and also the friction: every session begins by entering twelve or twenty-four words on a keyboard. That is slow, it is easy to mistype, and it is a moment where a camera or a person behind you matters. A hardware wallet asks for a PIN.

■ AND ONE THING NONE OF THE SIX FIXES

Most self-custodied bitcoin that gets lost is not stolen by an attacker. It is lost because the only backup was destroyed, thrown away, or never written down properly in the first place. Choosing between these six matters far less than writing your recovery words on something durable and keeping it where a fire or a burglary cannot take both it and you. Any of the six with a good backup beats the best of them without one.

04Which to pick

A short, opinionated answer

  1. Spending money — a few hundred. A hot wallet on your phone. The risk is bounded by the balance, and a setup you find annoying is one you will eventually use badly.
  2. Savings, and you would rather buy a solution. A hardware wallet from an established vendor, bought directly, checked on arrival. It is the least demanding way to get real physical protection, and there is no shame in it.
  3. Savings, and you would rather not trust a vendor. Tails with Electrum, or SignerOS. Both are free, both run on hardware you already have, and neither involves anyone shipping you anything. Tails has the review; SignerOS has the structural air gap and a machine that does one thing.
  4. Serious amounts. Multisig, with signers of different kinds — a hardware wallet and SignerOS, or two vendors and an offline PC. Then no single firmware bug, no single vendor and no single supply chain can take everything. SignerOS exports BIP48 cosigner keys for exactly this.
  5. You want to trust nobody at all. An offline PC with Bitcoin Core and your own node online. It is the most work by a wide margin and it is the only setup on this page where no third party is in the path at any point.

05Straight answers

Questions this page should not dodge

Is SignerOS safer than a hardware wallet?

Not in every way, and saying otherwise would be dishonest. It is stronger on supply chain (you buy nothing, so nothing can be tampered with in transit), on network isolation (no network code at all, rather than a stack left unplugged) and on leaving no trace (RAM only, forgotten at power-off).

A hardware wallet is stronger on physical security — a secure element, a PIN, wipe-after-N-attempts, a display that does one job — and on maturity, because established vendors have years of external review that a new project does not.

What is SignerOS actually the weakest at?

Independent review, physical protection, everyday convenience, and — for now — the reproducibility claim itself: the build has been measured on one machine, and nobody else's number has come back to confirm it. Those four are the section above, and they are the honest reasons to choose something else.

Is Tails with Electrum good enough instead?

For many people, yes. Tails is amnesic, widely used, and reviewed far more than this project is; Electrum's offline signing workflow is well established. The difference is that Tails has a full network stack and is designed to reach the internet through Tor, so an offline session is a discipline you maintain. SignerOS has no networking compiled in, which takes the discipline out of it — and takes every other use of that machine with it.

Can I use SignerOS together with a hardware wallet?

Yes, and it is one of the better uses of it. A multisig wallet whose signers are different kinds of device is not exposed to a single vendor, a single firmware bug or a single supply chain. SignerOS exports BIP48 cosigner keys for this, and verifies P2WSH and P2SH-P2WSH change against the witness script when it signs.

Is a hot wallet on my phone a bad idea?

Not for the amount you would carry in a pocket. Modern phones keep app secrets in hardware-backed storage and are patched more reliably than most desktops, so a good mobile wallet is a reasonable place for spending money. It is an online, general-purpose device running dozens of other programs, which is why it is a poor place for savings.

What does SignerOS not protect against?

Anything below the operating system on the machine you boot: compromised UEFI firmware, a management engine, hardware implanted in the computer itself. It does not protect a recovery phrase you have typed into a phone, photographed, or put in a cloud drive. It does not protect you from losing your only backup, which is how most self-custodied bitcoin is actually lost. And it cannot help if somebody is standing in front of you demanding that you sign.
